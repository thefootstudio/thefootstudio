Plantar Warts: What Works, What Doesn’t and When to See a Podiatrist

Plantar warts are one of the most common things we see, especially in kids and teens. They’re also one of the most frustrating. Here’s what they are, what the research says about treating them, and when it’s worth getting help.

What is a plantar wart?

A plantar wart (verruca) is a small growth on the sole of the foot caused by the human papillomavirus (HPV). The virus gets in through tiny breaks in the skin, often in warm, damp places like pool decks, gym showers and shared bathrooms.

Because they’re on the bottom of the foot, plantar warts are pushed inwards by your body weight, and hard skin often builds up over the top. That’s why they can feel like walking on a small stone.

Is it a wart or a corn?

Warts and corns can look very alike, but they’re treated very differently. A corn is caused by pressure. A wart is a viral infection. Some signs that point towards a wart:

  • tiny black dots in the middle, which are small blood vessels
  • skin lines that go around it, rather than through it
  • pain when it’s squeezed from the sides, rather than pressed straight on
  • a cluster of small warts close together (mosaic warts)

Getting this right first matters, so you don’t spend weeks treating the wrong thing.

Why do some people get them and others don’t?

Lots of people are exposed to the virus, but not everyone ends up with a wart. Your immune system plays a big part, which is why warts are so common in children, whose immune systems are still learning. Sweaty feet, cracked or broken skin, and walking barefoot in shared wet areas can all make it easier for the virus to take hold.

Do warts go away on their own?

Often, yes. Many warts clear up without treatment as the immune system catches up. In one study of children, around half of warts had gone within a year without treatment. Others can hang around for much longer, spread, or become painful enough to affect how you walk, play or exercise.

Choosing not to treat a wart that isn’t bothering you is a perfectly reasonable option, and we’re always happy to talk it through.

What does the research say about treatment?

Here’s the honest answer: there’s no single treatment that works for everyone, and plantar warts can be stubborn.

  • A Cochrane review of topical treatments found salicylic acid may work better than no active treatment, though the overall quality of the research is mixed.
  • There are lots of different treatment options for plantar warts, and everybody responds differently. The right option depends on how long the warts have been there, how widespread they are, whether there are other issues such as blood flow and healing capacity or systemic conditions, and whether the treatment is for a child.

That’s why we don’t believe in a one-size-fits-all approach. The right plan depends on the wart, how long it’s been there, how sore it is, your health, your activities, and for kids, their age and comfort.

How podiatrists can help

We start by checking it really is a wart. From there, we take a wider, holistic approach: we look at the whole person, your lifestyle and your whole foot, not just the wart. Often we’re treating other skin and nail issues at the same time. Depending on what suits you, options can include:

  • Watch and see: many warts clear on their own, so keeping an eye on a wart that isn’t causing problems is a reasonable first step.
  • Gentle home skin care: a simple, gentle routine to keep the skin around the wart healthy and comfortable while you wait or alongside treatment.
  • Debridement: gently reducing the hard skin over the wart, which eases pressure and helps treatments reach it.
  • Acid treatments and padding: applied with the healthy skin around the wart protected, with padding to take pressure off while you walk.
  • Falknor’s needling: an option for stubborn warts, done under local anaesthetic, which aims to help your immune system recognise and respond to the virus.
  • Working with your GP: if your immune system is affected, or other options haven’t worked.

We’ll also talk through over-the-counter options, and choosing not to treat. Read more about plantar wart treatment.

A word on home treatments

Over-the-counter wart treatments can help some people, but they need care. They can irritate or damage the healthy skin around the wart if they’re not used as directed. If you have diabetes, poor circulation, reduced feeling in your feet, or fragile skin, please don’t use them without speaking to a podiatrist or GP first.

Simple ways to lower your risk

  • Wear thongs or slides in communal showers, change rooms and around pools.
  • Keep your feet clean and dry, and change damp socks.
  • Don’t share towels, socks or shoes.
  • Don’t pick, scratch or cut a wart, as this can spread it.
  • Look after cracked or broken skin, so the virus has fewer ways in.

When to see a podiatrist

  • You’re not sure if it’s a wart or a corn.
  • It’s painful, or changing how you walk, run or play.
  • It’s spreading, or keeps coming back.
  • You have diabetes, poor circulation or a condition that affects your immune system.
  • It bleeds, changes colour or looks unusual.
